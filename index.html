01 — The work

Three ways people ask me in

Some come for private coaching. Some bring a leadership population, a culture, or a hiring problem. Some need a room of people to leave with language they can actually use.

For you

Empathy-Powered Leadership coaching

This is for kind, considerate, empathic professionals who want a calm, credible leadership style. You might be preparing for management, already leading, or working for yourself. You do not need a senior title to begin.

The pattern is familiar. People turn to you in a crisis, and then promote someone else. Your empathy is praised, and your judgement is overlooked. You are comfortable advocating for other people, and slower to negotiate your own fee, recognition, or next step. You do not need to harden to be taken seriously.

The named offer is a six-month, one-to-one package:

  • Pre-coaching work, including an online personality profile
  • Six sessions of 60 minutes, by video, with the option to record and replay
  • Integration tasks drawn from psychology, neuroscience, CBT, Internal Family Systems and self-compassion, shaped to your situation
  • Email and audio support between sessions
  • One follow-up session to look at what has changed

I also work in packages of 3, 6 or 9 sessions. We can meet face to face, or by video across time zones. Sessions can be weekly, fortnightly or monthly, between 8am and 9pm, and on Saturdays before 1pm. You keep a recording of each coaching session.

This is psychologically informed coaching. It is not therapy. Many people say the sessions feel therapeutic. The aim is different: evidence-based practice, a directive style, and something you can put to work.

For an organisation

Find the empathic people. Then let them lead.

You cannot train someone to care. Standard leadership programmes still try. I would rather identify the people who already notice others, and help them take up authority without burning out or disappearing.

Empathy-Powered Leadership Academy

A programme for the conscientious people who are already holding a team together, and who are rarely the ones put forward. It runs in three stages.

  1. Identify. Diagnostics, interviews and a baseline report.
  2. A 12-week academy. Live coaching pods, online content, practical work and reflection. Participants commit about 60 minutes in most weeks: a short video or audio, a worksheet, and a 30-minute pod. There are also six live group sessions of 60 minutes, scheduled ahead.
  3. Deliver. Follow-up learning, booster calls, and a report on what changed.

Sponsors in HR and the line have a light touch: a briefing at the start and a check-in part way through. The research I use with organisations finds that empathic leaders increase team engagement by 25%, strengthen organisational commitment by 20%, and are 11% less likely to burn out. They also make psychological safety, inclusion and innovation easier to sustain.

  • Assessment and selection — structured hiring, assessment centres and individual assessment

I use evidence-based methods, including cognitive behavioural approaches, mindfulness-based stress reduction and mindful self-compassion. I simplify them. People leave with something they can use at work and at home.

02 — The idea underneath the work

Empathy moves in two directions.

Compassion says: “How can I make it better?”

When empathy meets suffering, it becomes compassion. When empathy meets joy, it becomes what I call euphoresis — carrying someone’s good forward so it travels further than the moment.

The word is pronounced eu-FOR-eh-sis. It joins the Greek eu, for good, with pherein, to carry. I coined it because most workplace talk treats empathy as pain. Empathy is also the ability to recognise someone’s joy and let it move other people, ideas and momentum.

Both directions matter. One responds to what hurts. The other stops a culture from only noticing what is wrong.

03 — The book

The Super-Helper Syndrome

A survival guide for compassionate people. Written with Rod Vincent. We are both Chartered Psychologists and Associate Fellows of the British Psychological Society.

Super-Helper Syndrome is the name we gave to a state some people live in: helping others to the detriment of their own wellbeing. It is not a diagnosis or a personality type. It is a pattern. Helping is a serious part of being human, and of almost every job. It can also be the thing that empties you.

In the book we describe four adverse impacts: exhaustion, resentment, exploitation and self-criticism. Under those sit four beliefs we heard again and again.

  • The Good Person Belief. “I must help others to prove I am a good person.”
  • The Help Everyone Belief. “I must help everyone.”
  • The They-Couldn’t-Survive-Without-Me Belief.
  • The No Needs Belief. “I shouldn’t have any needs.”

The alternative we argue for is a Healthy Helper Mindset: your own needs count, criticism of yourself can be met with self-compassion, and boundaries make helping more effective, not less.

05 — About

A psychologist who stayed interested in good people

I wanted to be a psychologist from the age of twelve. I started in clinical work in the NHS. In 2004 I moved into the corporate world, and later worked as a senior HR manager in financial services through the credit crunch. I know what near-burnout feels like from the inside of a busy institution, not only from the literature.

The qualifications I work under are BSc, MSc, CPsychol, AFBPsS and CPBP. I am a Chartered Business Psychologist and an Associate Fellow of the British Psychological Society, and a Certified Principal Business Psychologist with the Association for Business Psychology. In 2021 I received a best coach award at the Business Women Awards. The coaching and the programmes draw on that training, on neuroscience, and on mindfulness-based stress reduction and mindful self-compassion. I also train and supervise other coaches.

Stand-up and improvisation are part of how I host a room. They are why a last-minute change of brief, including one twenty seconds before a live BBC World News interview, does not empty the session. The style people book again is relaxed, approachable, and still professional.

I am from south-east London. Until recently I was a trustee of BelEve UK, which builds leadership programmes for girls aged 8 to 18 in schools and communities there. Before that I volunteered for six years with SmartWorks as an interview coach. The practice itself is in Ludlow, Shropshire. Clients join from elsewhere, and I travel for in-person work.

With Rod Vincent, I also started Emplify. The line is simple: good people should have more power. You cannot train someone to care, so the work finds people who already do, and helps them be seen, hold their ground, and use authority in the service of others.
